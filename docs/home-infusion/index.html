Home IV antibiotics: a guide for patients and families

What happens when you finish your antibiotic treatment at home with Oahu Medical Group’s infusion nurses. Keep this page, or print it, next to your supplies.

Call the nurse on call right away if

  • Fever of 100.4 °F (38 °C) or higher, or shaking chills
  • Redness, swelling, pain, warmth or pus where the IV line enters the skin
  • Swelling or pain in the arm, shoulder or neck on the side of the line
  • The line is leaking, cracked, has pulled out partly, or won’t flush
  • Rash, itching, hives, or new diarrhea that is watery or frequent
  • You missed a dose, or a dose was damaged, late or not delivered

Call 911 for trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, chest pain, or if the line comes all the way out and bleeding won’t stop with firm pressure.

The on-call number is on your infusion instructions. A nurse answers 24 hours a day, 7 days a week.

How the program works

Your infectious disease physician has decided you need antibiotics that must be given into a vein, for a set number of weeks. Instead of staying in the hospital or going to an infusion center every day, you receive the doses at home. Oahu Medical Group prepares the doses, delivers them to you, and provides the nurses who teach you, visit you, and watch your progress. Your physician sees you in clinic during treatment and decides when to stop.

Your IV line

Most patients have a PICC line — a thin, soft tube placed in the upper arm that reaches a large vein near the heart — or a midline, a shorter version. It stays in for the whole course and is removed at the end. The line lets you receive medicine without repeated needle sticks, and blood for your weekly labs can usually be drawn from it.

  • Keep the dressing clean, dry and sealed. Your nurse changes it weekly, or sooner if it gets wet or loose.
  • Cover the arm with a waterproof sleeve or plastic wrap taped above and below for showers. No baths, swimming or ocean until the line is out.
  • Don’t lift more than about 10 pounds with that arm, and avoid blood pressure cuffs and needle sticks on it.
  • Flush the line as your nurse taught you, before and after each dose, to keep it open.

Giving the dose

At your first visit a nurse walks you — and a family member if possible — through every step, and watches you do it until you are comfortable. The method depends on the antibiotic: some run in from a small pump or ball-shaped infuser over 30 minutes to a few hours, some are pushed slowly by syringe, and some run continuously from a pump you carry. Your written instructions list your medicine, the dose, the exact times, and how long each infusion takes.   • Wash your hands and set up on a clean surface before every dose.
  • Check the label: your name, the medicine, and the date. Let a refrigerated dose come to room temperature as instructed.
  • Give doses on time. Timing matters more for some antibiotics than others; your instructions say how late is too late and what to do if you miss one.
  • Record each dose on your log sheet. Bring it to clinic.

Deliveries and supplies

Doses are delivered to your home on a schedule — usually weekly, sometimes more often for medicines that don’t keep long. Keep them in the refrigerator unless told otherwise, away from the freezer section, and don’t use a dose that looks cloudy, discolored or has particles in it. If a delivery hasn’t arrived when expected, call the office before you run out. Used needles and syringes go in the sharps container we give you; we take the full container back. Nurse visits and labs

A nurse visits at least weekly to change your dressing, check the line and draw blood. The blood tests look for side effects of the antibiotic on your kidneys, liver and blood counts, and for some medicines measure the drug level. Your physician reviews every result; we call you if a dose needs to change. Please keep your phone on and let us know if you will be away from home on a visit day.

Side effects

Each antibiotic has its own common side effects and your instructions describe yours. In general, mild nausea, a metallic taste, or soft stools are common and manageable; tell the nurse so we can help. Watery or frequent diarrhea, a rash, ringing in the ears, dizziness, or tingling in the hands and feet are reasons to call the same day. Never stop the antibiotic on your own — call us first.

Daily life

  • You can work, drive and walk as your energy allows; many people return to normal activity within the first week.
  • Alcohol: avoid it with certain antibiotics; your instructions will say. Ask before taking any new medicine or supplement, including over-the-counter ones.
  • Travel between islands is possible with planning; tell us at least a week ahead so doses and nursing can be arranged.
  • Keep every clinic appointment. Your physician decides the stop date based on how you are doing, the labs, and sometimes repeat imaging.

Finishing treatment

When the course is complete, a nurse removes the line — it takes a few minutes and is not painful — and you keep a small dressing on for a day. Your physician sees you in clinic a few weeks later to confirm the infection has stayed away. Call us after treatment ends if any of the original symptoms return.
