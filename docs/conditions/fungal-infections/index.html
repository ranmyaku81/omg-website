Fungal infections

Serious infections caused by yeasts and molds, usually in people with weakened immunity or after surgery.

What it is

Fungi are everywhere, and most fungal infections are minor, such as thrush or athlete’s foot. The infections we treat are the invasive ones: yeast (Candida) in the bloodstream or around an implant, molds such as Aspergillus in the lungs or sinuses, and Cryptococcus in the lungs or around the brain. They occur mainly in people whose immune system is weakened by chemotherapy, transplant medicines, steroids, uncontrolled diabetes or advanced HIV, and in people who have been very ill in intensive care.

How we diagnose it

Fungal infections can be hard to prove. We use blood cultures, blood tests that detect parts of the fungus, imaging, and often a biopsy or sample from the infected site. Because the fungi grow slowly in the laboratory, we sometimes have to start treatment before the results are final.

How we treat it

Antifungal medicines are chosen for the specific fungus and may be given by mouth or by IV, sometimes at home. Courses run from two weeks for a bloodstream yeast infection to many months for mold or Cryptococcus. Several antifungals interact with other medicines or need blood-level monitoring, so we review every medication you take and check levels and liver function during treatment. Removing an infected line or draining an abscess is often part of the plan, and we work with your other specialists to reduce immune-suppressing medicines where that is possible.

What to expect

We follow you closely during treatment, with imaging or blood tests repeated to confirm the infection is clearing. Dr. Majewski has a particular interest in fungal infections and sees many of these patients.

Call us if

  • Fever that returns during treatment
  • New headache, confusion, vision change or neck stiffness
  • Yellowing of the skin or eyes, or dark urine
  • Any new medicine started by another doctor, so we can check for interactions
